# EPCOT World Showcase: Documented Architectural Provenance

Research date: 2026-09-30. Scope: the eleven country pavilions of EPCOT's World Showcase.

## Method and source tiering

Counting rule applied throughout: a reference is **counted as an identifiable real building** only where a
source names a specific real structure (e.g. "Burg Eltz", "Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests"). Style
references ("Bavarian village", "Tudor", "Mesoamerican pyramid"), town references with no named building
("Taxco", "Covent Garden pubs"), landscape features, and designed objects that are not buildings
(telephone boxes, totem poles, gondolas, ships) are recorded separately and **not** counted.

Source tiers used below:
- **T1 Disney official**: D23 (Disney's official fan organization), Disney press/park material.
- **T2 Reference / published guidebook publisher**: Wikipedia (where cited), TouringPlans / The Unofficial
  Guides (Unofficial Guide series publisher), Disney historian Jim Korkis.
- **T3 Established fan reference sites**: AllEars.net, WDWInfo, MousePlanet, WorldOfWalt, WDW Magazine.
- **T4 Individual fan blogs**: e.g. eatwanderexplore.com, whereinthepark.com. Claims appearing **only** at
  T4 are flagged as unverified and are excluded from the counted totals.

Primary Disney official source used repeatedly:
https://d23.com/11-real-life-landmarks-that-inspired-epcot-world-showcase/

## 1. Mexico

### Named real building references

| Pavilion element | Real structure | Location | Source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exterior pyramid | El Castillo / Temple of Kukulcán | Chichén Itzá, Yucatán | https://d23.com/11-real-life-landmarks-that-inspired-epcot-world-showcase/ | T1 |
| Exterior pyramid (competing claim) | Temple of the Feathered Serpent / Temple of Quetzalcoatl | Teotihuacán, State of Mexico | https://allears.net/epcot/mexico-pavilion-world-showcase-epcot/ ; https://www.wdw-magazine.com/5-fascinating-facts-about-the-mexico-pavilion-at-epcot/ | T3 |
| San Ángel Inn Restaurante | San Ángel Inn (1692, former monastery) | San Ángel, Mexico City | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_Pavilion_at_Epcot ; https://www.wdw-magazine.com/5-fascinating-facts-about-the-mexico-pavilion-at-epcot/ | T2/T3 |

**Counted: 3** (the pyramid counts once; see dispute below).

### Source disagreement (significant)

The pyramid's model is **not settled**:
- D23 (Disney official) names **El Castillo at Chichén Itzá** (Maya).
- AllEars states "It is modeled after an Aztec Temple of Quetzalcoatl (the god of life) at Teotihuacan."
  WDW Magazine states "modeled after the Aztec Temple of the Feathered Serpent located in the ancient holy
  city of Teotihuacán."
- Both T3 claims contain a factual error in attribution: Teotihuacán is a pre-Aztec city (its builders are
  unidentified), so "Aztec temple at Teotihuacan" conflates two cultures. This error is repeated across fan
  sources, which suggests a single upstream source being copied.
- A T4 source (whereinthepark.com) states that official Disney materials describe the pyramid as "inspired
  by the architecture of three major pre-Columbian civilizations, the Maya, Aztec and Toltec," and breaks it
  down as Maya massing (El Castillo / Tikal proportions), Aztec serpent heads (Teotihuacán), and Toltec
  detail (Tula). This composite reading is consistent with both T1 and T3 claims being partly right, but the
  underlying Disney document is not independently verifiable here.
  https://shop.whereinthepark.com/blogs/where-in-the-park-podcast-blog/epcots-mexico-pavilion-exploring-the-architecture-culture-and-details

**Recorded position:** the pyramid is a documented Mesoamerican reference; which specific pyramid is
contested between Chichén Itzá (Yucatán, Maya) and Teotihuacán (Basin of Mexico).

### Style / place references not counted
- "Mesoamerican pyramid" generally (Wikipedia: "resembles a Mesoamerican pyramid with steps leading to
  entrance doors").
- Interior plaza said to be based on the silver-mining town of **Taxco, Guerrero** (T4 only, unverified). A
  town, not a named building.
- Temple of Quetzalcoatl at **Tula, Hidalgo** (Toltec) for detailing (T4 only, unverified).

### Non-building authenticity facts
- No documented Mexican government sponsorship or oversight of the design was found.
- A scholarly treatment exists (Latin American Research Review, Cambridge: "Recognizing and Representing
  Mexico at EPCOT Center's Mexico Pavilion") which notes Disney hired Mexican American artist Eddie Martinez
  and the Goez Art Studio of East Los Angeles. Full text was not accessible; only the abstract was read.
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/latin-american-research-review/article/recognizing-and-representing-mexico-at-epcot-centers-mexico-pavilion/DF7BEA0613475CD7368DEAF51A5CD169
- AllEars notes a "Bridal Couple" sculpture by a Mexico City family "whose family has been hand-making this
  type of celebratory art for more than 300 years." This is a merchandise/exhibit fact, not construction.

### Coherence judgment: NATIONAL PASTICHE

Regions involved: Yucatán (Maya, c. 800-1100 CE), Basin of Mexico / Teotihuacán (pre-Columbian, different
culture and millennium), Mexico City (1692 Spanish colonial), and, if the T4 claim holds, Taxco, Guerrero
(colonial silver town) and Tula, Hidalgo (Toltec). The pavilion places a pre-Columbian pyramid exterior over
a Spanish-colonial plaza interior, and its pyramid references are themselves drawn from sites in different
regions built by different civilizations centuries apart. This is a collage, not a portrait of one place.

## 2. Norway

### Named real building references

| Pavilion element | Real structure | Location | Source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stave church | **Gol Stave Church** (built 1157-1216) | Originally Gol, Hallingdal, Buskerud; relocated to the Norwegian Museum of Cultural History, Bygdøy, Oslo | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gol_Stave_Church ; https://allears.net/epcot/stave-church-norway-epcot/ ; https://theunofficialguides.com/2017/05/18/norway-pavilion/ | T2/T3 |
| Restaurant Akershus / Akershus Royal Banquet Hall | **Akershus Fortress / Castle** (14th c.) | Oslo | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway_Pavilion_at_Epcot ; https://theunofficialguides.com/2017/05/18/norway-pavilion/ | T2 |

**Counted: 2.**

Wikipedia's Gol Stave Church article states: "There is also a replica in the Scandinavian Heritage Park in
Minot, North Dakota and at Epcot Center in Walt Disney World in Florida," and "In the Norwegian Pavilion in
Epcot, Orlando, Gol Stave Church is Norway's landmark." D23's official piece names only the generic type
("stave churches, which were very common in Norway around the 13th century"), so the specific attribution
to Gol comes from T2/T3, not T1.

### Further named claims at T4 only (unverified, not counted)
- **Bryggen** wharf, Bergen (UNESCO site) as a model for shop facades.
- **Old Bergen Museum**, Bergen, as a shop facade model.
- **Detli House**, Sverresborg Trøndelag Folk Museum, Trondheim, as the Royal Sommerhus model.
  https://eatwanderexplore.com/blog/real-life-locations-epcot-world-showcase
  The Unofficial Guides corroborates only vaguely: "the inspiration for the Royal Sommerhus comes from a
  historical log cabin in Norway."

### Style references not counted (explicitly four regions)
Wikipedia and The Unofficial Guides both state the pavilion deliberately deploys four distinct regional
Norwegian building idioms, one per structure:
- **Setesdal** style (Puffin's Roost) - Setesdal valley, Agder
- **Bergen** style (Fjording / The Fjording shop) - Bergen, Vestland
- **Oslo** style (Akershus Royal Banquet Hall) - Oslo
- **Ålesund** style (Frozen Ever After building) - Ålesund, Møre og Romsdal (Jugendstil/Art Nouveau town)

### Non-building authenticity facts
- Not built or designed by the Norwegian state, but **Norwegian-funded**: Wikipedia states "it finally ended
  up with investors from Norway raising the US$30 million required to create an exclusive national
  pavilion," and "the federal government continued to contribute US$200,000 annually for five years" on the
  grounds that it was "a good promotional tool for their tourism industry."
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway_Pavilion_at_Epcot
- A full-scale Viking ship inspired by the **Oseberg ship** (excavated in Vestfold; original at the Viking
  Ship Museum, Oslo) was a former exhibit. A ship, not a building, so not counted.
- No imported materials or imported artisans documented.

### Coherence judgment: NATIONAL PASTICHE (and explicitly designed as one)

Regions involved: Hallingdal/Buskerud (stave church), Oslo (Akershus, Oslo-style), Bergen/Vestland,
Setesdal/Agder, Ålesund/Møre og Romsdal, plus Trøndelag if the Royal Sommerhus claim holds. Norway is the
clearest documented case of intentional pastiche: sources describe the design brief as assigning a different
regional style to each building, so the village depicts no single Norwegian town.

## 3. China

### Named real building references

| Pavilion element | Real structure | Location | Source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Circular theatre building (Reflections of China / Wondrous China) | **Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests (Qinian Dian)**, in the Temple of Heaven complex, erected early 1400s | Temple of Heaven Park, Beijing | https://d23.com/11-real-life-landmarks-that-inspired-epcot-world-showcase/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China_Pavilion_at_Epcot | T1/T2 |

**Counted: 1 firmly documented.**

D23 (official) calls it "a replica of a structure erected in Beijing, China, in the early 1400s." Wikipedia:
"The courtyard is dominated by a replica of the Temple of Heaven." Both T1 and T2 name the Temple of Heaven;
the specific building within that complex (the Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests / Qinian Dian, also
transliterated Chi Nien Tien) is identified at T3/T4.

### Further named claims at T4 only (unverified, not counted)
- Entrance gate based on the **Yunhui Yuyu Archway** ("Glowing Clouds and Holy Land" archway) at the
  **Summer Palace**, Beijing; also given as the Paiyun Gate.
- **Lotus Blossom Café** resembling the **Longevity Chamber** at the Summer Palace, Beijing.
  https://shop.whereinthepark.com/blogs/where-in-the-park-podcast-blog/exploring-epcot-s-china-pavilion-architecture-legends-and-hidden-details
  https://eatwanderexplore.com/blog/real-life-locations-epcot-world-showcase
  Both are plausible and specific, but no T1-T3 source was found naming them, so they are recorded as
  unverified.

### Style references not counted
- **Forbidden City**, Beijing: named as a general stylistic source ("the architecture in this pavilion is
  primarily from ancient China, specifically the Forbidden City and the Temple of Heaven"), T4.
- **Suzhou classical gardens**, Jiangsu, as the idiom for the pavilion's gardens (T4). Named individual
  gardens (Humble Administrator's Garden, Lion Grove, Lingering Garden) are offered only as examples of the
  type, not as reproduced structures.
- The **Terracotta Army** of Xi'an, Shaanxi, appeared as a travelling/loan exhibit. An artefact display, not
  architecture.

### Non-building authenticity facts
- No documented Chinese government funding or design oversight was found. Wikipedia's article contains no
  statement on imported materials, Chinese artisans, or government involvement.
- Sponsorship history is corporate/commercial, not governmental, unlike Morocco.

### Coherence judgment: REGIONALLY COHERENT (Beijing imperial), with one garden-idiom outlier

Regions involved: Beijing, for every named building reference (Temple of Heaven, and, if the T4 claims hold,
two Summer Palace structures and the Forbidden City idiom). The one departure is the **Suzhou** (Jiangsu)
garden vocabulary, roughly 1,000 km south, and that is a landscape style rather than a reproduced building.
On named buildings alone, China is the most regionally concentrated pavilion in World Showcase: it presents
imperial Beijing specifically, not a survey of China.

| Das Kaufhaus (art/book shop facade) | **Historisches Kaufhaus**, a 16th-century merchants' hall | Freiburg im Breisgau, Baden-Württemberg (Black Forest) | https://touringplans.com/blog/five-things-to-know-about-the-germany-pavilion-in-epcot/ ; https://allears.net/epcot/germany-epcot/ | T2/T3 |
| Backdrop wall / castle facade | **Burg Eltz** (Eltz Castle) on the Mosel | Wierschem, Rhineland-Palatinate | https://allears.net/epcot/germany-epcot/ | T3 |

| One exterior facade | A ~400-year-old **town hall** on a square in **Frankfurt** | Frankfurt, Hesse | https://allears.net/epcot/germany-epcot/ | T3 |
| Chiming clock / Glockenspiel | **Rathaus-Glockenspiel**, Marienplatz | Munich, Bavaria | https://touringplans.com/blog/five-things-to-know-about-the-germany-pavilion-in-epcot/ | T2 |

**Counted: 6** (5 buildings/castles plus the St. George fountain-statue, which is a specific identifiable
structure rather than a style).

### Source problems and disagreements (significant)
- **Frankfurt town hall, garbled name.** AllEars reads: "An exterior facade was copied from a 400-year old
  town hall in Romsburg Square in Frankfurt." There is no "Romsburg Square" in Frankfurt. This is almost
  certainly a corruption of **Römerberg**, the square holding the **Römer**, Frankfurt's historic town hall.
  The claim is recorded here as a Frankfurt town hall reference with the caveat that the source text is
  corrupt and the exact building name is unconfirmed.
- **Glockenspiel, two competing models.** TouringPlans (T2) attributes the pavilion clock to "Munich's famous
  Glockenspiel." A T4 source instead attributes the clock tower to the **Schwabentor** and **Martinstor**
  gate towers of Freiburg im Breisgau. These are incompatible attributions (Munich vs Freiburg); the T2
  Munich claim is the better-sourced one and is the one counted.
- **Röderbogen and Markusturm**, Rothenburg ob der Tauber, are claimed as the Biergarten exterior model at
  T4 only. Not counted. AllEars supports only the weaker statement that "an indoor German 'courtyard'
  reflects the 16th century town of Rothenberg."
- **Explicit source caution against over-reading.** TouringPlans states plainly of the pavilion's buildings:
  "Most of the buildings aren't duplicates of real buildings elsewhere." This is an important corrective:
  the German pavilion is predominantly invented in a regional idiom, with a handful of specific quotations.

### Style references not counted
- Generic medieval/Bavarian village architecture, with Rothenburg ob der Tauber as the overall mood source.
- Wikipedia: the pavilion uses "architecture from different eras and regions."
- The never-built boat ride was to have shown the Rhine, Tauber, Ruhr and Isar rivers, with a miniature
  **Cologne Cathedral**. Unbuilt, so not counted.

### Non-building authenticity facts
- No documented imported materials, imported artisans, or German government or corporate-national design
  oversight was found in T1-T3 sources.

### Coherence judgment: NATIONAL PASTICHE

Regions involved: Bavaria (Rothenburg ob der Tauber, Munich), Baden-Württemberg / Black Forest (Freiburg),
Rhineland-Palatinate twice over on two different rivers (Burg Eltz on the Mosel, Burg Stahleck on the
Rhine), and Hesse (Frankfurt). Five or more separate regions, several hundred kilometres apart, are quoted
into one square. Sources state the intent directly: the buildings mix "different eras and regions," and most
are not duplicates of anything real. Germany is a pastiche both regionally and chronologically.

### Named real building references

| La Gemma Elegante shop / plaza centrepiece | **Doge's Palace (Palazzo Ducale)**, 14th c. | Venice, Veneto | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy_Pavilion_at_Epcot ; https://touringplans.com/blog/five-things-to-know-about-the-italy-pavilion-in-epcot/ | T2 |

| Fontana di Nettuno | A Roman Neptune fountain (see dispute) | Rome, Lazio | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy_Pavilion_at_Epcot ; https://www.wdwinfo.com/wdwinfo/guides/epcot/epws-italy.htm | T2/T3 |

**Counted: 5.**

### Source disagreement
The Fontana di Nettuno's model is inconsistently reported:
- Wikipedia and TouringPlans: "reminiscent of Rome's **Trevi Fountain**" / "meant to evoke the famous Trevi
  Fountain in Rome."
- WDWInfo and a T4 source: inspired by **Bernini's Neptune fountain**, with the T4 source specifically
  placing it at **Piazza Navona**.
- Note a factual wrinkle in the second claim: the Fontana del Nettuno in Piazza Navona was completed in the
  19th century by Antonio Della Bitta, not by Bernini (Bernini's Navona work is the Fontana dei Quattro
  Fiumi). Sources invoking "Bernini's Neptune fountain" are therefore imprecise.
- **Recorded position:** a Roman fountain reference is documented; which Roman fountain is contested, and the
  Bernini attribution appears to be an error.

### Style references not counted
- The pavilion overall "is set up to evoke the feeling of St. Mark's Square in Venice" (a square, not a
  single building).
- **Naples and Florence** dining/architecture idioms for the Via Napoli restaurant (opened 2010), per
  Wikipedia: "inspired by the dining/architecture trademarks of Naples and Florence." No specific Neapolitan
  or Florentine building is named.
- Via Napoli's three pizza ovens are named for the volcanoes Etna, Vesuvius and Stromboli. Not buildings.
- A hand-painted **Carretto Siciliano** (Sicilian cart) display. An object, not architecture.

### Non-building authenticity facts
- Wikipedia: "Venetian Mask Artisans from Balocoloc in Venice, Italy have also been featured in this
  Pavilion since 2001." A resident-artisan programme, not construction.
- Wikipedia: "Sbandieratori di Sansepolcro, a flag throwing show performed by authentic artists from
  Sansepolcro, Italy (since October 2014)." Performance, not construction.
- Wikipedia notes water imported from Pennsylvania for Via Napoli's pizza dough to approximate Neapolitan
  water chemistry. This is a food-preparation fact, not building provenance, and the imported item is
  American rather than Italian.
- No documented Italian government design oversight, imported building materials, or Italian construction
  artisans were found.

### Coherence judgment: REGIONALLY COHERENT (Venetian), with one Roman outlier

Regions involved: Veneto/Venice supplies four of the five named building references, and all of them come
from one square and its immediate waterfront (Piazza San Marco, the Piazzetta, the Palazzo Ducale, the Ponte
della Paglia). The single outlier is the Roman fountain. The Naples/Florence/Sicily material is a later
overlay (Via Napoli, 2010) expressed as style and decor rather than named buildings. Italy is therefore the
strongest case for regional coherence among the eleven: the original design reproduces a specific, tightly
bounded Venetian precinct.

## 6. The American Adventure

### Named real building references

| Pavilion element | Real structure | Location | Source | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main pavilion building (Georgian style) | **Independence Hall** | Philadelphia, Pennsylvania | https://d23.com/11-real-life-landmarks-that-inspired-epcot-world-showcase/ | T1 |
| Main pavilion building (composite) | **Monticello** (Thomas Jefferson's house) | Charlottesville, Virginia | https://allears.net/2010/03/08/a-walking-tour-of-epcots-american-adventure-pavilion/ | T3 |
| Main pavilion building (composite) | **Old State House** | Boston, Massachusetts | https://allears.net/2010/03/08/a-walking-tour-of-epcots-american-adventure-pavilion/ | T3 |
| Main pavilion building (composite) | **Colonial Williamsburg** buildings | Williamsburg, Virginia | https://allears.net/2010/03/08/a-walking-tour-of-epcots-american-adventure-pavilion/ | T3 |

**Counted: 4** (Colonial Williamsburg is a district rather than one building; it is counted here because the
source names it as a discrete architectural source alongside the three named buildings, but it is the
weakest of the four as an "identifiable building.")

D23 (official) names only Independence Hall: the pavilion is "designed in the Georgian style that can be
seen in Philadelphia's Independence Hall." The four-way composite comes from AllEars, which lists "the
classic Georgian style of the late 1700s, Colonial Williamsburg, Independence Hall, Thomas Jefferson's
Monticello, and the Old State House in Boston."

### Further named claim at T4 only (unverified, not counted)
- Interior rotunda modelled on the **United States Capitol Rotunda**, Washington, D.C.
  https://eatwanderexplore.com/blog/real-life-locations-epcot-world-showcase

### Style references not counted
- Colonial / Georgian architecture of the late 1700s, as a period idiom.

### Non-building authenticity facts (construction technique, well documented)
- **Handmade brick:** AllEars reports "handmade bricks made of Georgian clay, 110,000 of them," deliberately
  made to "appear to be over 200 years old."
  https://allears.net/2010/03/08/a-walking-tour-of-epcots-american-adventure-pavilion/
- **Forced perspective:** "By using forced perspective, the Imagineers made the building appear to be about
  two and a half stories high, when in actuality it's five stories tall." The building is cited at
  108,000 sq ft.
- These are the two most-repeated construction facts for this pavilion. Note the material is domestic
  (Georgia clay), so this is a fidelity-of-technique fact rather than an imported-material fact.

### Coherence judgment: NATIONAL PASTICHE, but stylistically unified

Regions involved: Pennsylvania (Independence Hall), Virginia twice (Monticello, Colonial Williamsburg),
Massachusetts (Old State House), and, if the T4 claim holds, the District of Columbia. This is a composite of
buildings from four or five separate states, so it is a pastiche by the same test applied to Germany and
Norway.

The important qualification: unlike Germany or Norway, all the sources sit inside a single architectural
idiom (Anglo-American Georgian/Colonial of the Atlantic seaboard, roughly 1750-1800). The result reads as one
coherent building because the quoted sources are stylistically homogeneous even though they are
geographically dispersed. Recorded as: geographically a pastiche, stylistically coherent.

Notes on sourcing strength:
- D23 (official) confirms only the Itsukushima torii. Wikipedia confirms the torii and the Hōryū-ji pagoda:
  "The entrance to the courtyard features a Japanese pagoda based on the Hōryū-ji Temple" and "A torii gate,
  modelled after that of Itsukushima Shrine, decorates the water in front of the pavilion."
- The Shishinden, Himeji and Katsura attributions are T3. They are internally corroborated: the pavilion's
  castle is signed in-park as "Shirasagi-jō", which is Himeji Castle's own byname, so the Himeji attribution
  is well supported even though it rests on T3 sources.
- The Katsura Grill claim is scoped carefully by its source: "designed to resemble a tea house that might be
  found in the Katsura Imperial Villa in Kyoto." That is a type-within-a-named-place reference rather than a
  reproduction of one specific teahouse. Counted, with that caveat.

- Wikipedia: "Disney worked closely with Japanese architects to ensure the accuracy of the buildings."
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Pavilion_at_Epcot
- The pavilion's retail and restaurant operations were sponsored by **Mitsukoshi**, a Japanese department
  store company. Commercial sponsorship, not documented design oversight of the pavilion architecture.
- No imported building materials or imported construction artisans were documented in the sources found.

### Coherence judgment: NATIONAL PASTICHE

Regions involved: Nara Prefecture (Hōryū-ji pagoda), Hiroshima Prefecture / Miyajima (Itsukushima torii),
Kyoto twice (Gosho Shishinden, Katsura Imperial Villa), and Hyōgo Prefecture (Himeji Castle). Four
prefectures, spanning roughly 300 km, and four different building types from different centuries (7th-8th
century temple architecture, a 12th-16th century shrine gate, a 9th-century-founded imperial palace hall
rebuilt in the 19th century, a 17th-century villa, and a 17th-century castle). Japan is a pastiche, though a
Kansai-weighted one: three of the five sources (Nara, Kyoto x2, Hyōgo) are in the Kansai/Kinki region.

Sourcing is unusually good here: D23 (Disney official) confirms the Koutoubia Minaret ("a replica for the
icon located at Koutoubia Mosque in Marrakech"); Wikipedia confirms Koutoubia, Bab Boujeloud and the Chellah
("a replication of the necropolis in Rabat"); and Disney historian Jim Korkis confirms all five with
locations.

### Non-building authenticity facts (the strongest documented set of any pavilion)
- **National government sponsorship, unique in World Showcase.** Wikipedia: "the country's government aided
  in the design," and "The government also sponsored the pavilion, while a corporation holds sponsoring
  rights on every other pavilion." TouringPlans: "This is the only pavilion in the World Showcase where the
  country's government assisted with the design, materials, and craftsmen." Government sponsorship ended
  21 October 2020, when Disney assumed ownership.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morocco_Pavilion_at_Epcot
- **King Hassan II's personal involvement.** Korkis: King Hassan II "felt there needed to be a pavilion
  representing the culture of Africa," funded the pavilion, and sent his own artisans.
- **Named artisan headcount.** Korkis reports the king sent **eight plaster craftsmen, eleven tile craftsmen
  and two wood craftsmen** (21 in total) to Florida. Wikipedia states "King Hassan II sent his artisans to
  Florida to construct the entire pavilion; the hand-tiling performed by his craftsmen can be seen in the
  restaurant."
  https://yourfirstvisit.net/2017/08/25/a-friday-visit-with-jim-korkis-morocco-in-epcots-world-showcase/
- **Imported materials.** Korkis: "Nine tons of handmade, hand-cut tiles were used, the same kind that had
  been used to decorate palaces of past kings of Morocco."
- **Religious/artistic constraint observed.** Wikipedia notes the Moroccan artisans' mosaics contain no human
  representations, in keeping with Islamic artistic tradition.
- **Later alteration.** A 2024 refresh of the fountain and courtyard replaced some of the original tilework
  with machine-made tile, which fan coverage treated as a loss of the original artisan work.
  https://blogmickey.com/2024/05/authenticity-lost-morocco-pavilion-fountain-courtyard-receives-pedestrian-tilework-following-refresh/
  (T4 source; reported here as a change to the fabric, not as a provenance claim.)

### Coherence judgment: NATIONAL PASTICHE, tightly bounded

Regions involved: Marrakesh (Koutoubia Minaret), Fez three times (Bab Boujeloud, Nejjarine Fountain, Dar
al-Magana), and Rabat (Chellah Minaret). Three cities, several hundred kilometres apart, so by the test used
throughout this document Morocco is a pastiche rather than a portrait of one city.

Two qualifications worth recording. First, the pastiche is narrow: all three cities are Moroccan **imperial
cities**, and all five named references are in a continuous Moro-Andalusian architectural tradition, so the
collage is stylistically consistent in a way Germany's and Mexico's are not. Second, Fez supplies three of
the five references, making Fez the dominant single source.

### Important scoping correction
Wikipedia's France pavilion article lists the **cliffs of Étretat** (Haute-Normandie), **Mont Saint-Michel**
(Normandy), the **Château de Chambord** (Loire Valley) and **Notre-Dame de Paris** in its description of the
film *Impressions de France*, not of the pavilion's architecture. A careless read of that article produces
four false architectural references. On re-checking, Wikipedia's only architectural statement is that the
pavilion is "themed to look like a Parisian neighborhood with a pool and fountains and with a view of the
Eiffel Tower in the distance."
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France_Pavilion_at_Epcot
These four are film content and are excluded here.

### Further named claim at T4 only (unverified, not counted)
- The pavilion's fountain as a miniature of the **Fountain of Warsaw** in the Jardins du Trocadéro, Paris.
  https://eatwanderexplore.com/blog/real-life-locations-epcot-world-showcase

### Style and non-architectural references not counted
- **Belle Époque** Paris (dated 1871-1914 by AllEars), including the period's widened boulevards and
  seven-storey height limit, as the pavilion's governing period idiom.
- The waterside area inspired by Georges Seurat's painting *A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande
  Jatte*. A painting, and its subject (Île de la Jatte, Neuilly-sur-Seine) is a place, not a building.

### Non-building authenticity facts
- **Eiffel Tower construction method:** AllEars reports the replica is roughly 1/10 scale, 69 ft tall on a
  34 ft building, uses forced perspective, and that "The Imagineers used blueprints from Gustave Eiffel's
  1889 original to design their model," down to miniature elevators and turn-of-the-century beacon lights.
  This is the best-documented instance of a pavilion working from a real building's original drawings.
  https://allears.net/2011/09/03/epcots-france-pavilion-part-one/
- The Notre-Dame gargoyle in the Palais du Cinema lobby is reported as a direct casting of the original.
- Les Chefs de France was opened by French chefs Paul Bocuse, Roger Vergé and Gaston Lenôtre (Wikipedia).
  A culinary credential, not building provenance.
- No French government funding or design oversight was documented.

### Coherence judgment: REGIONALLY COHERENT

Regions involved: Paris supplies every counted reference (Eiffel Tower, Les Halles, Pont des Arts,
Notre-Dame). The only non-Paris candidate, the Fontainebleau theatre, is uncounted for lack of a named
building, and Fontainebleau is in any case within Île-de-France, about 60 km from Paris. Combined with an
explicit single-period frame (Belle Époque, 1871-1914), France presents one city at one moment rather than a
survey of the country. Along with Italy and China, this is one of the three regionally coherent pavilions.

architecture, landscaping, and even climate zones."

Canada is a pastiche that sequences a west-coast wilderness front to an eastern urban rear, spanning roughly
3,500 km, and its two named references (Ottawa and Victoria) sit at opposite ends of the country.

### Cross-cutting notes
- **Three pavilions are regionally coherent**: China (Beijing), Italy (Venice), France (Paris). Each presents
  one city, and in France's case one city at one named period.
- **Morocco is the outlier on documented authenticity**, not on coherence. It is the only pavilion with a
  national government as sponsor and design collaborator, the only one with a documented headcount of
  artisans sent by that government (21, per Korkis), and the only one with a documented tonnage of imported
  traditional material (nine tons of hand-cut tile).
- **Two pavilions were designed as pastiches on purpose and say so**: Norway (a different named regional
  style per building) and Canada (a west-to-east wilderness-to-city sequence).
- **Sourcing quality varies sharply by pavilion.** Morocco, Italy, Japan and Germany have multiple specific,
  well-corroborated named references. The UK and Canada have very few named buildings and are mostly style
  compositions. Mexico has the single worst-corroborated headline claim in World Showcase: its own official
  and fan sources name two different pyramids in two different regions.
- **Two recurring research traps** were found and are documented above: Wikipedia's France article lists film
  locations in a way that reads as architecture, and fan sources repeat an "Aztec temple at Teotihuacán"
  formulation that conflates two cultures.
